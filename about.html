About

I am a literacy and information scholar interested in how children, families, educators, and community institutions make learning possible across languages, cultures, and everyday experiences.

My work sits at the intersection of literacy studies, sociolinguistics, teacher education, and library and information science. Across these areas, I am interested in a common question: How do we learn to recognize forms of knowledge, participation, and competence that often go unnoticed?

From Classroom Teacher to Researcher

Before becoming a university professor, I taught English language arts in South Korea for six years. I worked with students across elementary, middle, and high school settings. Those years continue to shape how I think about literacy, teaching, and educational equity.

As a classroom teacher, I became increasingly interested in the experiences students brought with them into schools. I often found myself wondering why some forms of knowledge were recognized and valued while others remained invisible. Those questions eventually led me to pursue doctoral study in the United States.

Language, Migration, and Family

My move to the United States introduced new questions about language, identity, and belonging. As an immigrant, multilingual speaker, and later a parent of bilingual children, I became increasingly interested in how families navigate educational institutions across languages and cultures.

Many of the questions that drive my scholarship emerged from everyday experiences: conversations in museums, visits to libraries, interactions in classrooms, and family moments that revealed how learning happens beyond formal schooling.

Why Museums and Libraries?

Some of the most important educational experiences occur outside schools. Museums, libraries, and community spaces provide opportunities for children and families to explore ideas, ask questions, tell stories, and build knowledge together.

These settings have become central to my research because they reveal forms of participation that are often overlooked in traditional educational contexts. They also offer possibilities for imagining more inclusive and humanizing institutions.

Beyond Academia

Outside of research and teaching, I enjoy exploring museums, libraries, parks, and community spaces with my family. Many of the ideas that inspire my scholarship begin in these everyday experiences.

I continue to see scholarship as a collaborative endeavor shaped by conversations with children, families, educators, librarians, and community members. My hope is that research can contribute not only to academic knowledge, but also to more equitable opportunities for participation and belonging.
